# Accuracy classes and maximum permissible errors for trade measuring instruments

Accuracy classes and maximum permissible errors that the law assigns to instruments used for trade, from three authorities that do not publish a common table: Directive 2014/32/EU (the EU Measuring Instruments Directive), Directive 2014/31/EU and OIML R 76-1 (non-automatic weighing instruments), and NIST Handbook 44 code 2.20 (scales, United States). Each record is one instrument type at one accuracy class under one measurement condition, with the permitted error that authority states. The MID side covers nine of the ten instrument-specific annexes: water meters (MI-001), gas meters and volume conversion devices (MI-002), thermal energy meters (MI-004), measuring systems for liquids other than water including fuel dispensers and road tankers (MI-005), automatic weighing instruments (MI-006), taximeters (MI-007), material measures (MI-008), dimensional measuring instruments (MI-009) and exhaust gas analysers (MI-010). Active electrical energy meters (MI-003) carry no records, and that is a statement about the source rather than an omission: Annex V sets its limits in a Table 2 whose load-current bands EUR-Lex renders as images, so neither the band nor the value it pairs with is in the page's text and no row can be keyed to it honestly. Annex VI is thin for the same reason -- every formula in it is an image, so only the flow sensor's 5 % cap, which the directive states in words, is here. Answers 'what is the maximum permissible error for a water meter', 'what accuracy class does a petrol pump have to be', 'MID accuracy class for a taximeter', 'allowed error on a weighbridge', 'thermal energy meter accuracy class 2 MPE', 'is a class III scale legal for trade in both the EU and the US' and 'is my fuel dispenser within tolerance'. The values are condition-dependent -- the error allowed at low flow differs from the error allowed at high flow, and in-service limits differ from new-instrument limits -- so a single percentage figure is almost always the wrong answer. The weighing records are what makes this a juxtaposition rather than a restatement: for a non-automatic weighing instrument the EU directive and OIML R 76-1 state the same three limits over the same load bands, while NIST Handbook 44 adds a fourth band to classes III and IIII and carries a class III L that exists nowhere in Europe. No single document says that, and getting it today means reading a 443,000-character standard, a 300,000-character directive and a 470,000-character handbook side by side.

Every record carries `source` (the page it came from) and `source_quote` (the exact line on that page which states it), so any value here can be checked without asking us. Where a source does not state something the row is omitted rather than guessed.

**Licence position for this dataset.** EU legislation reused under Commission Decision 2011/833/EU on the reuse of documents from the EU institutions. legislation.gov.uk content is Open Government Licence v3.0. NIST Handbook 44 is a work of the United States Government and carries no copyright in the US. OIML R 76-1 states no licence -- OIML publishes its recommendations as free downloads rather than selling them, but it is still a body whose product is partly the compilation, so what is taken here is the fact plus one short attributed quote linking back to the PDF, never the document. The single-publisher volume advisory that applied while this was EU-only is weaker now that three authorities supply the records, and the two largest are legislation published under an explicit reuse decision or free of copyright outright.
